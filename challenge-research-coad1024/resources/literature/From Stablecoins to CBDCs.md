# From Stablecoins to CBDCs: The Public Benefits of a Public-Private Partnership

**Authors:** Christian Catalini, Wanyi Dai Li, Alonso de Gortari, Andrew Lilley  
**Date:** December 16, 2021

## Abstract

Stablecoins can support low-cost, instantaneous payment rails that are interoperable and programmable. This can increase competition in financial services, reduce the cost of accepting digital payments for small and medium businesses, and foster greater financial inclusion among segments that are currently unbanked. The public sector and non-profits can also take advantage of the technology to implement more effective economic stimulus, conditional cash transfers, and benefit programs. While stablecoins are often seen as competing with central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), both approaches to digital and programmable money are complements, and could therefore not only co-exist, but also extend each other based on the relative strengths of public and private sector.

---

## 1 Introduction

What does the future of money look like? The question touches on key aspects of how payments, banking and other financial services work today, including what type of entities are allowed to compete and under what regulatory framework. While proponents of the status quo argue that the current system works just fine (Eichengreen, 2021), in reality the United States severely lags behind the global frontier (Catalini and Lilley, 2021).

But what’s the best way to close the gap and deliver on a more competitive and inclusive financial system? Proponents of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) such as ECB president Lagarde believe that the public sector should take a “leading role in ensuring that payments remain efficient, safe and inclusive in the digital age” and that “given developments we see, not so much in bitcoin but in stablecoins projects... we’d better be ahead of the curve”. At the same time, CBDC skeptics argue that the private sector, under the right regulatory framework, may be in a better position to deliver on many of the same objectives. In the words of Federal Reserve Vice-Chairman Quarles, “the combination of imminent improvements in the existing payments system [...] combined with the cross-border efficiency of properly structured stablecoins could well make superfluous any effort to develop a CBDC.”

Both sides make valid points, and given the rapid progress in China and a strong sense of urgency in the United States, it should come as good news that we do not have to choose between mutually exclusive paths after all. CBDCs and stablecoins should not only co-exist, but can actually complement each other in a way that plays to the relative strengths of public and private sector. Modern money has long been a collaboration between central banks and financial institutions, and stablecoins and CBDCs can extend this public-private partnership in a way that fosters more choice for consumers and businesses, as well as better public services (Catalini and Massari, 2021).

This “upgrade” of our money system could be phased in progressively, and in a way that does not compromise the safety, integrity, and stability of the financial system. The upside is the ability to build a truly interoperable, lower friction, and more competitive payments and financial system where stablecoins can offer programmable rails not only for instantaneous payments, but also for new types of products and services.

Under such an approach, the public sector could focus on ensuring the stability of money, providing a robust regulatory framework for stablecoin issuers, and then, as needed, deploying a CBDC to directly address market failures and ensure that everyone still has access to a central bank liability in the form of digital cash. The private sector, instead, could focus on developing new business models and products that compete with incumbents by unbundling services related to payments, credit and banking that have been intertwined for decades.

This two-pronged strategy would combine the relative strengths of the public and private sector, reduce time to market, ensure new entrants can grow and thrive, and give central banks enough time to progressively solve the challenges a CBDC otherwise poses. Central banks would also reduce the risk of wasting cycles on the development of a top-down solution that, when eventually ready, may not meet the needs of the market.

But what would a hybrid, two-pronged solution look like? And what benefits could it entail? We now turn to how a public-private partnership could work in this setting, and what the upside would be from a public sector perspective.

## 2 Setting Standards for Stablecoins

While the vast majority of CBDC projects remain in the early ideation stage, over $100 billion worth of stablecoins are already in circulation. Yet, not all stablecoins are created equal. Private stablecoins are only as strong as their reserve design, and substantial work is needed to improve the quality of the backing of some of the largest stablecoins available (see Catalini and de Gortari (2021) and Catalini and Shah (2021)). Since the economics supporting a stablecoin’s peg boil down to guaranteeing that each coin can be redeemed at par at any point in time, unless issuers become fully-fledged banks and issue “deposit coins”, a 1:1 backed reserve is a critical component of any robust design (Catalini and Massari, 2021). But full-backing during regular market conditions is hardly enough. Redemption should be guaranteed even during market stress. A reserve that risks becoming less-than-fully-backed tomorrow, will likely face a run today as stablecoin holders rush to redeem coins before the reserve is depleted.

Many existing stablecoin reserves are unlikely to maintain their value during a financial crisis, rendering the current use of the word stablecoin a misnomer. In practice, existing stablecoins encompass a wide range of designs that differ substantially in terms of the quality of their backing. Among the riskiest models, algorithmic stablecoins build on assets that could become completely worthless in a run. These designs are so fragile that runs can materialize even under the healthiest market conditions, and could be triggered by a change in expectations about the future success of the stablecoin’s ecosystem (Catalini and de Gortari, 2021).

In light of these facts, Federal Reserve Governor Brainard has rightly noted that the current ‘stablecoin’ landscape risks resembling “the period in the nineteenth century when there was active competition among issuers of private paper banknotes in the United States.” A period “now notorious for inefficiency, fraud, and instability in the payments system.” Governor Brainard emphasized that this period “led to the need for a uniform form of money backed by the national government.”

But stablecoins can be built using robust economic design (Catalini et al., 2021), and this constitutes an opportunity rather than a threat for the public sector as it begins its journey towards programmable money. To start with, regulators could ensure that private stablecoins adopt safe reserve designs and appropriate capital buffers, regularly disclose their reserve composition, and operate with high levels of transparency (Catalini and Shah, 2021). For example, when it comes to reserve design, the Diem project introduced an approach that maximizes both stability and liquidity by focusing on short-maturity, high-quality liquid assets (3 months or less U.S. Treasuries). This design was successful in pushing large private stablecoin issuers to start their migration towards safer and more transparent approaches (Koning, 2021). Promoting such a model as the standard for fully-backed stablecoins would encourage their use as a safe, efficient, and high-velocity medium of exchange.

More generally, the public sector could act as a standard setter that upholds consumer protection and financial stability. Promoting a safe reserve backing is only a first step in this direction. Other areas that will need better standards and regulatory frameworks are anti-money laundering, countering the financing of terrorism, the prevention of illicit activity. The public sector should also ensure that consumers have a meaningful choice when it comes to protecting the privacy of their transactions (see Athey et al. (2017) for some of the key challenges). This extends beyond private entities such as wallets and exchanges and is also important for a future migration to a CBDC. As noted by economist Darell Duffie: “The greatest challenge for CBDC designers is protecting the privacy of transactions while at the same time effectively monitoring payments for their legality, particularly with respect to money laundering and financing terrorism.”

All of these goals can be pursued in a technology neutral way to preserve optionality and avoid selecting winners and losers. To ensure healthy competition between traditional and new approaches, the public sector can also enforce interoperability between traditional and new payment networks (Catalini and Kominers, 2021). By reducing the competitive advantage of incumbents and the role of established network effects, interoperability is a powerful equalizer between large and small players.

## 3 The Public Benefits of Stablecoins

Digital payments are an integral part of our lives, and the Covid-19 pandemic has made their importance even more salient. While technologies from contactless payments to mobile wallets have offered socially-distant ways to pay local and online businesses, there is still a substantial need to make them more affordable for low income and unbanked households, as well as for millions of small and medium businesses (SMB). Low income households experience higher banking costs and transaction fees, with 13.8% of Black and 12.2% of Hispanic households among the unbanked in 2019, compared to 2.5% for White households (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, 2020). Small and medium businesses bear the costs of accepting digital payments through high interchange fees to card issuing banks (1.15% to more than 3%), assessment fees to card networks (0.13 to 0.15%) and additional fees collected by payment processors (which vary drastically based on the type of merchant and transaction – see (The Ascent, 2020)).

Stablecoins, if well designed, have the potential to substantially reduce these frictions and inefficiencies. They can operate real-time payments at a lower cost relative to legacy payment systems, and outside of traditional banking hours. This can make payments and financial services more accessible to segments of the population that are currently underbanked or unbanked. Similarly, they can reduce costs for small businesses and make funds available to them within shorter time frames.

Last, stablecoins can support applications beyond basic digital payments through programmability. While the industry has mostly focused on decentralized finance (“DeFi”) applications, programmability can unlock meaningful new use cases for the public sector, which we briefly discuss next.

### 3.1 Economic Stimulus

During the pandemic, many governments faced significant barriers in delivering fast and secure stimulus to the economy. While directed fiscal transfers have been a frequent tool in recessions to increase consumer confidence and support the economy, governments still face significant barriers in ensuring the fast and secure distribution of these funds. There is also an opportunity to improve the effectiveness of these interventions with targeted approaches that more rapidly boost consumption.

A significant portion of the US population receives stimulus via paper checks, which are slow, expensive and insecure. In the 2008 recession, for example, it took approximately 3 months for recipients to access funds through direct deposit, and 5 months if they did not have access to a bank account (York, 2020). This reduces the impact of stimulus packages as lower income households do not receive relief fast enough. Roughly 18% of the 2020-2021 Economic Impact Payments was done through paper checks, some checks were fraudulently rerouted using unauthorized postal address changes, and others were sent to the wrong recipients because of database mistakes (IRS, 2020; Menton, 2021).

Stablecoins could improve the timeliness and accuracy of stimulus transfers. By allowing recipients to use digital wallets that follow robust identity and compliance standards, missed payments and fraud can be reduced, and unbanked recipients can avoid paying check-processing fees (often more than 2% of the disbursed funds) and receive their funds instantaneously.

Stablecoins could also increase the efficiency of government spending. Stimulus payments are designed to encourage consumer spending and drive revenues for businesses, lifting the economy. However, this is often not the case. For example, 80% of the 2001 and 2008 stimulus recipients invested their funds, and research shows that the marginal impact of each $1 of stimulus payment was estimated to be only $0.33 (Shapiro and Slemrod, 2009). Results for 2020 are comparable, with Coibion et al. (2020) finding that approximately 40% percent of the total transfer was spent on average. On programmable payment rails, governments could introduce expiration dates and allowable categories to increase the marginal propensity to consume stimulus funds.

### 3.2 Public Benefit Programs

While stimulus packages are rare, most US government public benefit programs support individuals and households in need on a much more regular basis. Programs including social security benefits, Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) are typically only paid out monthly, but this cadence is likely too infrequent.

The SNAP program, for example, spent $86 billion in 2020, and this represented a large 35% increase in spending because of more families struggling through the pandemic (Roof, 2020). Research has revealed that SNAP recipients decrease their caloric intake by 10-15% over the course of a month (Shapiro, 2005), with 16.5% of their benefit being spent on the first day, and 89.1% in the first three weeks (Castner et al., 2020). Approximately 12% of households exhaust their benefits in the first week, 23% in the first two weeks, and 33% in the first three weeks (Castner et al., 2020). More importantly, the low frequency of distribution has consequences beyond irregular nutrition: children’s test scores are lower near the end of a SNAP benefit cycle (Cotti et al., 2018), and school disciplinary infractions increase, especially among male students (Gennetian et al., 2016).

Similar cyclical behavioral effects have been observed with social security benefits. For example, drug-related hospitalizations and deaths increase after a SSI disbursement, with researchers attributing this to a “full wallet” effect (Dobkin and Puller, 2007). Stablecoins can lower the cost of more frequent payments, helping recipients smooth consumption and avoid these negative consequences.

### 3.3 Conditional Cash Transfers

Conditional cash transfer programs have been an invaluable tool for governments and non-profit organizations throughout the world to incentivize behavioral changes in healthcare, education, public safety, and more. Within healthcare, they are an effective way to increase the uptake of preventative services (Lagarde et al., 2009). In many developing countries where education attainment is low (notably, the Mexican Progresa/Oportunidades program – see Parker and Todd (2017)), cash benefits are provided for school attendance and healthcare for children. These transfers not only induce short term behavioral changes, but also improve long term outcomes such as labor force participation and earning potential, disrupting intergenerational poverty (Millán et al., 2019).

However, conditional cash transfer programs are expensive to implement. After initial pilots are completed, and the right targeting strategies and interventions have been identified, programs often face challenges with scaling disbursements (Mercy Corps, 2017). Handing cash to participants requires additional staffing, monitoring, and verification to prevent fraud. As with public benefits programs, the programmability of stablecoins combined with digital wallets allows program sponsors to target participants based on a variety of demographic features, further improving efficiency and effectiveness. Apart from targeting specific demographics, programmability also allows linking disbursements with the completion of required actions.

## 4 From Stablecoins to CBDCs

The same public benefits of stablecoins described in the previous section could be achieved through the deployment of a central bank digital currency. But the approaches should not be seen as mutually exclusive. Once stablecoins are growing under robust, safe and compliant standards, and multiple stablecoin networks are successfully competing for adoption, central banks could identify areas of improvement and clear market failures to focus their CBDC development efforts on. If a CBDC is indeed needed to address key concerns related to privacy, universal access, market stability, competition, or more, then central banks could consider issuing their own digital and programmable coins to the public as a direct liability. This would scale down the role of stablecoin issuers as managers of reserves, as the CBDC coin would be superior even to privately issued ones backed by government debt only. Unlike government debt – and assuming CBDC coins do not carry a substantially less favorable interest rate – a CBDC would not have any underlying liquidity or market risk. Under such a scenario, stablecoin issuers could focus on the payment rails as well as building out the critical interface between the CBDC and different consumer and business applications.

In such a model, the central bank would not need to necessarily run a full retail payments ledger, and could open the ability to mint and burn CBDC coins to regulated intermediaries including banks, stablecoin operators, other fintech platforms, digital wallets, etc. Including non-bank institutions in this mix ensures that competition for new products and services can thrive, and that digital payments and related applications are not only deployed by the incumbents.

### 4.1 Towards a Renewed Public-Private Partnership

When it comes to money and payments, the public and private sectors have complemented each other successfully for decades. The majority of payments made in the United States today run on private rails, and the bedrock of the US payments system – ACH – was built in collaboration with banks and through a standard setting body known as Nacha. Nacha partners with government agencies including the Federal Reserve and the US Treasury to ensure the sound functioning of the payments system, and Federal Reserve member banks process a large share of all ACH payments.

Public-private partnerships have played a key role in promoting innovation and building new ecosystems before, as with the internet’s transition from an initially publicly funded project to its subsequent commercial success. DARPA’s key role in developing TCP/IP – the basic structure for sending and receiving information across a computer network – later became the technological backbone that enabled a multitude of commercial web applications. By setting the right standards and ensuring that private sector participants can successfully build new types of consumer and business applications, the public sector can recreate this dynamic. This would not only guarantee that resources invested towards a CBDC are well spent, but also that stablecoin ecosystems follow high standards for consumer protection, financial stability, market integrity, interoperability, competition, and operational resilience.

Space transportation provides a useful analogy here: initially entirely led by the public sector and delivering on a number of successful missions (e.g. Apollo program), the industry subsequently waned until a new wave of startups emerged in the early 2000s. Thanks to public-private partnerships such as the NASA-SpaceX one, the sector witnessed a sharp decline in payload costs and a massive influx of new ideas and approaches. Stablecoins provide a similar opportunity for bringing back competition and innovation to the financial sector.
